The Damage Starts Before the First Breath

By Dr. Barry Dublin, MD  |  The Mitochondrial Decline Series

Part 1: Power Plants Part 2: Before the First Breath Part 3: Six Doorways Part 4: Adults & Slow Burn Part 5: The Fix

If mitochondria are the body's power plants, then the question that should haunt every parent, every pediatrician, and every honest physician right now is this: at what age does the damage actually begin?

The answer, supported by a growing pile of human research, is uncomfortable. It begins before the child is born.

For a long time, the inside of the womb was treated almost like a sealed sanctuary. People imagined the placenta as a kind of filter that screened out the bad stuff and let in only the nutrients and oxygen the baby needed. That picture is no longer accurate. The placenta is more like a permeable membrane than a fortress, and many of the chemicals modern humans encounter every day are now showing up on the wrong side of it. Not in trace amounts. Not in unusual cases. In nearly every sample tested, in study after study, across continents.

Microplastics in Every Placenta Tested

In one widely discussed study, researchers analyzed sixty-two human placentas. Every single one contained microplastics — particles so small they had migrated out of food, water, dust, and air, traveled through the mother's bloodstream, and lodged in the tissue that was nourishing her unborn baby. Not most of them. Every single one. The most commonly identified type of plastic in those samples was polyethylene, the everyday plastic used to make grocery bags, food packaging, and countless consumer products.

Whatever you imagined about the protective barrier between modern industrial chemistry and the developing fetus, that finding should change your mental picture forever.

PFAS in Cord Blood — Before the First Meal

The story does not stop with plastics. There is a class of chemicals called per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, abbreviated PFAS, and nicknamed forever chemicals because they break down so slowly that they accumulate in water, food, and human bodies for decades. PFAS are the chemistry behind non-stick cookware, grease-resistant fast-food wrappers, stain-resistant carpets and couches, waterproof outdoor gear, and a long list of consumer products built on the idea that we want surfaces nothing sticks to.

The trade-off is that the chemicals that make a pan non-stick or a couch stain-proof do not stay where they were applied. They migrate into water, food, dust, and eventually people. PFAS are now detectable in the blood of virtually every American who has ever been tested. They cross into breast milk. They show up in the first stool a newborn ever passes, called meconium — which means the baby was exposed before ever eating anything from the outside world. Recent advanced testing has identified dozens of different PFAS compounds in cord blood, far more than older studies could measure.

Measuring Mitochondrial Damage Before Birth

Once you accept that these chemicals are already inside pregnant women, inside the placenta, and inside newborns, the next question is the obvious one: are they actually damaging anything?

The honest scientific answer is yes, and the proof is no longer indirect. Researchers can now measure mitochondrial damage in babies before they are even born, using a marker called mitochondrial DNA copy number, or mtDNAcn. Every cell has some baseline number of mitochondrial DNA copies. Stress, injury, or toxic exposure can shift that number. Lower numbers in newborn cord blood often indicate that the mitochondria are running on depleted reserve. Higher numbers can indicate that stressed mitochondria are trying to compensate. Either direction, when it deviates from the expected pattern, suggests biological strain.

In a landmark prenatal study, researchers found that for every increase of ten micrograms per cubic meter in late-pregnancy exposure to PM2.5 — meaning fine particulate pollution from traffic, combustion, smoke, and industrial emissions — there was an 8.55 percent decrease in cord-blood mitochondrial DNA copy number. A baby whose mother breathed dirtier air during pregnancy was born with measurably less mitochondrial signal in her blood. The baby had never taken a single breath outside the womb. The damage was already biologically logged.

PFAS, Children, and a Clean Dose-Response

A 2025 study examined prenatal PFAS exposure and newborn mitochondrial DNA. It found that higher prenatal PFAS exposure was associated with lower mitochondrial DNA copy number at birth — the same pattern observed with air pollution, but from a completely different chemical class.

A 2025 panel study from two Chinese cities measured eleven specific PFAS chemicals alongside leukocyte mitochondrial DNA copy number in 284 children aged four to twelve, over three seasonal sampling rounds. The result was a clean dose-response curve: every doubling of blood PFOA — one of the most common PFAS — was associated with a 6.36 percent decrease in mitochondrial DNA copy number. The effect was stronger in older children and in those with reduced kidney function — meaning the children least able to clear the toxin showed the most damage.

A 2023 study found that short-term exposure to a mixture of metals — nickel, manganese, and antimony, the kind of mixture that does not even make headlines — was associated with lower mitochondrial DNA copy number in healthy children with no known disease. Not children from contaminated industrial sites. Healthy children carrying the everyday mixed-metal load that is now part of life in many places.

Green Space, Pollution, and the Mitochondrial Signal

Then there is the green-space data, which is one of the most compelling parts of the whole picture. A 2025 study from China examined 1,151 children and adolescents aged six to eighteen and measured how much green vegetation surrounded their homes using satellite imagery. The result: more residential greenery was associated with higher leukocyte mitochondrial DNA copy number. The protective benefit was weakened in children also living with secondhand smoke exposure. A similar finding was observed in Belgian primary school children — greater surrounding green space was linked to higher buccal mitochondrial DNA content, while children in heavily urbanized settings had lower readings.

Notably, children living near intensive agricultural areas did not benefit the same way despite living surrounded by visible vegetation — almost certainly because pesticide exposure was offsetting whatever advantage the greenness might otherwise have offered.

In completely different parts of the world, using different methods, in different age groups, researchers are seeing the same pattern: the more polluted and chemically saturated a child's environment is, the worse their mitochondrial readings tend to look. The more biologically clean and natural the environment is, the better those readings tend to be.

The Columbia University Kraków Comparison

Now consider the most powerful piece of comparative evidence in the entire field. For decades, the Columbia Center for Children's Environmental Health has run parallel birth cohorts in both New York City and Kraków, Poland, specifically measuring combustion-related air pollution exposure in pregnant women and the resulting DNA damage in their newborns' cord blood.

Pregnant women in Kraków were exposed to roughly ten times higher levels of airborne polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons — the chemical residue of incomplete combustion — than pregnant women in New York City. In both cities, researchers measured PAH-DNA damage markers in newborn cord blood. The finding that should haunt anyone who reads it: in the New York City research, newborns showed approximately ten times higher levels of PAH-related DNA damage per unit of exposure than the mothers themselves.

The same dirty air injured the developing fetus far more severely than it injured the adult breathing it. The fetus is not just along for the ride. The fetus is, in many ways, far more biologically vulnerable to the very same exposure than the mother carrying it.

The Population-Level Numbers

A 2025 paper published in Academic Pediatrics found that the prevalence of chronic conditions or functional limitations in American children aged five to seventeen rose from about 22.6 percent in 1999–2000 to over 30.2 percent by 2017–2018 — approximately 130,000 additional affected American children every single year. A separate 2025 analysis in JAMA found chronic conditions rose from 39.9 percent in 2011 to 45.7 percent by 2023. The CDC now estimates roughly one in 31 American children is on the autism spectrum and that more than 7 million American children have ADHD. United States children also die younger, more often, than children in other wealthy nations — and the gap has widened.

You can stack genetic explanations against this curve, but they do not fit. Human genetics does not shift this fast. What has changed is not the genes. What has changed is the environment those genes are developing inside of.

The Shared Biological Fingerprint

A 2024 systematic review found that autism spectrum disorder, ADHD, intellectual disability, speech delay, and anxiety in children share a remarkably consistent biological fingerprint: increased oxidative stress, elevated toxic metal burden, decreased methylation capacity, mitochondrial dysfunction, and reduced blood flow to the brain. These are not five completely separate diseases with five completely separate causes. They appear, in substantial and growing measure, to be five different expressions of the same underlying cellular energy crisis, driven by cumulative mitochondrial erosion during critical developmental windows.

This is the mechanism through which the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease framework — known as DOHaD — explains why one prenatal exposure can leave biological fingerprints still measurable in a child at age nine, or fourteen, or beyond. The damage is not just in the moment. It is in the programming.

The Same Biology That Can Be Damaged Can Also Be Rebuilt

That picture is heavy, but it is also the beginning of the way out. The same biology that can be damaged can also be supported and rebuilt. Mitochondria respond to demand. They respond to clean inputs. They respond to good fuel. They respond to good sleep. They respond to less chemical and inflammatory load. The body is not a one-way road into illness. It is a system that listens to what you give it.

The point of understanding all of this is not to be afraid. It is to know what is happening so that you can give your body, and your children's bodies, the conditions to heal.
